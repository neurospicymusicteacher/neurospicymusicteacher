When a Sticker Isn’t a Support

“What if another sticker isn’t the missing piece?” A sticker won’t make the room quieter. Let’s figure out what’s actually getting in the way.

By Sarah Batchelor · Draft created · Sources checked 25 November 2026

A note about my perspective: I'm a music educator, special education specialist, neurodivergent human and parent — not a medical professional, psychologist or neuroscientist. I'm just a neurospicy person with intense hyperfocus, professional and lived experience, and access to the internet.

My articles explore research through an educational and lived-experience lens. They are intended to inform, encourage curiosity and offer practical ideas, not to provide clinical advice. I encourage you to explore the original research linked throughout.

If the barrier is that the room hurts your ears, a sticker does not make it quieter.

That is the distinction I want us to consider before designing another reward chart. An incentive may change how attractive an outcome looks. An accommodation changes something about access. Sometimes a student needs instruction. Sometimes they need a different environment. Sometimes more than one thing is true.

This is not a declaration that every sticker is harmful. Some people enjoy collecting them. The question is what adults expect the sticker to accomplish and what happens when it does not.

Start with the barrier

Suppose a student does not begin a practice task. In this illustrative example, the sheet says “practise the hard bit,” but the student cannot identify a starting point. Offering a prize for completion leaves the instruction problem intact.

A useful support might name the section, demonstrate a strategy and agree on a short first attempt. Another student might need a quieter space, an accessible score or help assembling an instrument. The same visible behaviour can involve different barriers.

Ask before deciding. A student may know exactly what would help, or may need time and a different way to explain. “I don't know” can be a starting point for collaborative observation.

What reward research does and does not say

Deci, Koestner and Ryan's meta-analysis found that some expected tangible reward arrangements reduced measures of intrinsic motivation, while positive feedback could have different effects. Conditions and outcome measures matter. The work does not support the blanket statement that all praise or every reward harms every learner.

It also does not establish what a particular school chart will do for a student with a PDA profile. My recommendation is to examine the actual arrangement: is it experienced as encouragement, information, pressure or a public record of difficulty?

A chart can record a barrier without removing it

A public display may tell everyone who completed a task. It tells us much less about access, effort, pain or the amount of support available. Repeatedly recording non-completion can become an elaborate way of documenting that the plan is not working.

Use private, descriptive feedback where possible. “The visual cue helped you find the entrance” offers information. “You earned being a good student today” entangles the task with moral worth.

Specific feedback can still be unwelcome when someone is under pressure. Ask how the student prefers to receive it. Support does not become responsive simply because the adult intends it kindly.

Protect what the student needs

Do not make food, communication tools, necessary breaks or agreed accommodations contingent on earning points. If music is part of a support plan, removing it because the student struggled elsewhere can remove the very route the plan was meant to provide.

Safety and supervision still matter. If an activity cannot happen safely, explain the immediate limit and arrange another way to meet the need. That differs from withholding support to create motivation.

Review the plan, not just the points

Ask whether the student can access the task more independently, understands the goal and feels able to seek help. Ask about the cost at home as well as the behaviour visible at school.

If an incentive is used, keep reviewing it with the learner. Does it help? Does it add pressure? Is it covering up a barrier that adults could change?

A sticker can be a small pleasure. It should not have to do the work of clear instruction, an accessible environment or a relationship in which asking for support is safe.

Sources and evidence limits
