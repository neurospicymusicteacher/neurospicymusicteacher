Failure First: Mistakes Are Data

“What if getting it wrong was part of the plan?” Wrong notes give us something to work with. Let’s talk about learning without the shame spiral.

By Sarah Batchelor · Draft created · Sources checked 25 November 2026

A note about my perspective: I'm a music educator, special education specialist, neurodivergent human and parent — not a medical professional, psychologist or neuroscientist. I'm just a neurospicy person with intense hyperfocus, professional and lived experience, and access to the internet.

My articles explore research through an educational and lived-experience lens. They are intended to inform, encourage curiosity and offer practical ideas, not to provide clinical advice. I encourage you to explore the original research linked throughout.

A wrong note is information.
It is not a character reference.

That distinction is the starting point for my Failure First approach. We build mistakes into learning as normal, expected and useful. We remove the moral judgement that can turn “that entrance was late” into “I am a disappointment.” Then we teach what to do next.

Failure First is my educational approach, informed by related research. It is not an independently validated intervention. That matters because a promising connection between ideas is different from evidence that a particular classroom model produces a particular outcome.

How I introduce it: “You’re wearing a red shirt.”

When I introduce this idea to students, I choose a student I know is comfortable being singled out for a lighthearted example. Then I start with something ordinary:

“You’re wearing a red shirt. Does that mean you have a good sense of fashion? A bad sense of fashion? Neither. It tells us you’re wearing a red shirt.”

That’s it. A fact does not always need a good-or-bad label attached to it. Sometimes it can just be a fact.

“So when I say, ‘That should be F-sharp,’ am I saying you’re a terrible musician? No. I’m saying it should be F-sharp. Fix it. We have things to play.”

The correction is about the note. It is not a review of your entire personality.

“As your director, it is literally my job to correct you until you can correct yourself. So yes, I’m going to point out mistakes. A lot. I’m going to make mistakes, too, and we’re going to acknowledge those as well. We can’t improve something if we won’t acknowledge what happened.”

The point is not to pretend that musical accuracy doesn’t matter. The note still needs to change. The point is to stop making every correction carry a second, unnecessary message about who you are.

“That should be F-sharp” can mean exactly that. Information. Something we can work with. Not a personal attack.

As a practical consideration for other educators, this example depends on the student feeling comfortable with the attention. Use yourself as the example if that is a better fit for the group. The point is to remove judgement, so the introduction needs to feel respectful, too.

What happens after the mistake?

Imagine a rehearsal where a student misses an entrance. This is an illustrative example, not a report about a particular student. The teacher could demand more effort. Or the teacher could ask: “Where did you lose your place? Would a cue, a marked rest or a slower rehearsal help?”

The musical goal stays visible. The next step becomes teachable. A student can take responsibility for trying a strategy without accepting a judgement about their worth.

That is more useful than announcing that mistakes are welcome and then reacting with visible frustration when they arrive. Students experience the error climate through what adults and peers actually do.

Where the research connects

Research on classroom error climate followed students in mathematics classrooms and linked a more positive perceived climate with less deterioration in constructive responses to errors over time. This was an observational study in mathematics, not a test of Failure First in music.

Error management training research also offers a useful connection: encouraging exploration and learning from errors can help people apply skills in new situations. Its settings differ from a school band room, so I treat it as related evidence rather than a ready-made music curriculum.

Teach the recovery, too

My proposed routine is simple: notice, describe, choose, retry and reflect. “I am terrible” becomes “I lost the pulse during the rest.” “Try harder” becomes “Tap the pulse while someone else plays, then try the entrance.”

An adult can model this with their own ordinary musical mistake. Describe what happened, name a strategy and demonstrate another attempt. There is no need to stage a humiliating failure or require students to perform errors for everyone else's benefit.

A student who is already overwhelmed may need a pause, privacy or a smaller task before another attempt. Repetition without support can become repeated evidence that the environment is unsafe. Recovery includes having permission to stop and return later.

High expectations need usable routes

Removing shame does not remove feedback. A rhythm can still need work. An unsafe action can still require a boundary and repair. The distinction is between describing an action and defining a person by it.

Self-compassion experiments challenge the assumption that harsh self-criticism is necessary for improvement. They provide a reason to question shame as a motivational tool; they do not prove that a classroom phrase will change a child's mental health.

For families, a useful meeting question is: “What happens here when my child gets something wrong?” For educators: “Have I taught a recovery strategy as explicitly as I taught the notes?”

The aspiration is a rehearsal room where a mistake leads somewhere: information, a strategy, another possibility. Students deserve an education in how to learn, including when learning sounds unfinished.

Sources and evidence limits

  • Dresel et al. (2025; first online 2024). Learning from errors in mathematics classrooms: Development over 2 years in dependence of perceived error climate. British Journal of Educational Psychology, 95, 180–196. Longitudinal observational study
  • Keith & Frese (2008). Effectiveness of error management training: A meta-analysis. Journal of Applied Psychology, 93, 59–69. Meta-analysis
  • Breines & Chen (2012). Self-compassion increases self-improvement motivation. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 38, 1133–1143. Four experiments
