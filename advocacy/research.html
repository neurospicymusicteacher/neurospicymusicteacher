Research, sources and evidence limits

A shared bibliography for the articles and advocacy resources.

Checked 25 November 2026. These are selected relevant sources, not a complete systematic review. Verification below identifies the material inspected; abstract checks do not imply a full-text appraisal.

Keep the claims separate. Failure First is Sarah’s proposed educational approach. Singing is not established as making worry impossible. Music education, listening and clinical music therapy are distinct. No source here establishes a music treatment for PDA.
Longitudinal observational study

Dresel et al. (2025; first online 2024). Learning from errors in mathematics classrooms: Development over 2 years in dependence of perceived error climate. British Journal of Educational Psychology, 95, 180–196.

1,641 students in 69 mathematics classrooms over two years. Positive perceived error climate was associated with less negative development of adaptive error responses. Association in mathematics does not validate Failure First in music.

Verification scope: Full text · 25 November 2026

Meta-analysis

Keith & Frese (2008). Effectiveness of error management training: A meta-analysis. Journal of Applied Psychology, 93, 59–69.

24 studies, 2,183 participants; positive overall effects, especially on adaptive transfer. Skills-training contexts differ from school music and PDA supports.

Verification scope: Abstract · 25 November 2026

Four experiments

Breines & Chen (2012). Self-compassion increases self-improvement motivation. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 38, 1133–1143.

Compassionate responses to personal shortcomings increased measures of motivation to improve. Not a school music intervention or a long-term student mental-health outcome.

Verification scope: Abstract and author-hosted paper · 25 November 2026

Adult singing study

Schladt et al. (2017). Choir versus Solo Singing: Effects on Mood, and Salivary Oxytocin and Cortisol Concentrations. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 11, 430.

Reported worry decreased after singing. Adult choir participants; no demonstration that worry is impossible during singing, and no PDA-specific treatment finding.

Verification scope: Full text · 25 November 2026

Three laboratory experiments

Schendel & Palmer (2007). Suppression effects on musical and verbal memory. Memory & Cognition, 35, 640–650.

Speaking and singing interfered with sequence-recognition memory. A plausible cognitive connection, not evidence of clinical rumination reduction.

Verification scope: Abstract · 25 November 2026

Randomized experiment

Radstaak et al. (2014). Music and psychophysiological recovery from stress. Psychosomatic Medicine, 76, 529–537.

123 participants; mood improved with music, but rumination did not significantly differ between recovery conditions. Listening is not equivalent to singing or instrumental participation.

Verification scope: Abstract · 25 November 2026

Theoretical research review

Watkins (2008). Constructive and unconstructive repetitive thought. Psychological Bulletin, 134, 163–206.

Distinguishes helpful and unhelpful repetitive thought. Does not imply that every neurodivergent person has rumination or an inner verbal monologue.

Verification scope: Full-text record · 25 November 2026

Laboratory experiments

Taruffi et al. (2017). Effects of Sad and Happy Music on Mind-Wandering and the Default Mode Network. Scientific Reports, 7, 14396.

Music conditions affected mind-wandering and associated brain activity. Mind-wandering is not synonymous with rumination; this is not a treatment study.

Verification scope: Abstract · 25 November 2026

Laboratory listening study

Koelsch et al. (2019). Heroic music stimulates empowering thoughts during mind-wandering. Scientific Reports, 9, 10317.

62 participants; music influenced reported thought content. Immediate thought content does not establish lasting self-concept change.

Verification scope: Full text · 25 November 2026

Systematic review

Kildahl et al. (2021). Pathological demand avoidance in children and adolescents: A systematic review. Autism, 25, 2162–2176.

13 studies; significant methodological limitations constrain conclusions about the proposed profile. Do not present autonomy supports as a proven PDA treatment.

Verification scope: Abstract and publisher findings · 25 November 2026

Systematic review of diagnostic evidence

Company & Rotella (2026). A Systematic Review of Pathological Demand Avoidance (PDA): A Veritable Diagnosis or a Case of Circular Logic? Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders.

12 studies; inconsistent identification and high methodological risk of bias. Limits support for diagnostic tools and distinct diagnosis; does not justify dismissing an individual’s distress.

Verification scope: Full text · 25 November 2026

Participatory qualitative research

Raymaker et al. (2020). “Having All of Your Internal Resources Exhausted Beyond Measure and Being Left with No Clean-Up Crew”: Defining Autistic Burnout. Autism in Adulthood, 2, 132–143.

Adult accounts describe exhaustion, loss of skills and reduced tolerance to stimuli. Not a diagnosis of a child’s day-to-day fluctuation.

Verification scope: Full-text record · 25 November 2026

Historical theory

Maslow (1943). A theory of human motivation. Psychological Review, 50, 370–396.

Describes relative satisfaction of needs and exceptions to rigid ordering. “Maslow before Bloom” is educational shorthand, not an experimentally established learning sequence.

Verification scope: Original paper · 25 November 2026

Meta-analysis

Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999). A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation. Psychological Bulletin, 125, 627–668.

128 experiments; effects vary with reward arrangements and measures. Does not mean every reward, sticker or form of praise is harmful.

Verification scope: Abstract and author-hosted paper · 25 November 2026

Systematic review and meta-analysis

Jamey et al. (2024). Does music training improve inhibition control in children? A systematic review and meta-analysis. Cognition, 252, 105913.

22 controlled longitudinal studies, including eight randomized trials; benefits on inhibitory control. Not evidence of universal executive-function or academic improvement, or a PDA treatment.

Verification scope: Abstract · 25 November 2026

Review of a longitudinal program

Habibi et al. (2018). Music training and child development: A review of recent findings from a longitudinal study. Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences. DOI: 10.1111/nyas.13606.

Musically relevant auditory skills and related brain changes after sustained training. Broad academic gains cannot be inferred from brain differences.

Verification scope: Abstract · 25 November 2026

Systematic review and meta-analyses

de Witte et al. (2020; first online 2019). Effects of music interventions on stress-related outcomes: A systematic review and two meta-analyses. Health Psychology Review, 14, 294–324.

104 randomized trials across varied settings; average reductions in psychological and physiological stress measures. Not a guarantee for any individual or school setting.

Verification scope: Abstract · 25 November 2026

Cochrane systematic review

Geretsegger et al. (2022). Music therapy for autistic people. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, CD004381.

26 studies; probable benefits for overall improvement and quality of life. Social interaction and communication findings remain uncertain. Clinical music therapy is distinct from classroom music.

Verification scope: Review and plain-language summary · 25 November 2026

Meta-analysis

Sala & Gobet (2020). Cognitive and academic benefits of music training with children: A multilevel meta-analysis. Memory & Cognition, 48, 1429–1441.

Broad cognitive and academic transfer was null after accounting for design quality. Include this counterpoint alongside more specific positive findings.

Verification scope: Full text · 25 November 2026

Practice framework, not a research study

CAST (2024). Universal Design for Learning Guidelines, version 3.0.

Options in engagement, representation, and action or expression. Used as design guidance; not evidence that a particular music support works.

Verification scope: Official guidance · 25 November 2026

Emerging research held for further verification

Løberg & Koelsch (2026), Music as mindfulness: Focused listening reduces negative mind-wandering and strengthens attention networks, was discussed earlier. A preprint record was located, but the original manuscript was not accessible during this check. It is not used to support the articles or packet. Preprint DOI for follow-up.

Further education and practical tools

PDA Society offers community and professional information; it is distinct from the original studies above. Canadian Association of Music Therapists explains professional music therapy. The detailed toolkit retains the free and low-cost music tools.
