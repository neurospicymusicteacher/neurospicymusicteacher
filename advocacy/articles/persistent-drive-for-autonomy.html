PDA, language & autonomy

Why Do I Call PDA a Persistent Drive for Autonomy?

“What changes when we start with autonomy?” Why these words shape the questions I ask and the support I want schools to provide.

By Sarah Batchelor · 25 November 2026

Pathological Demand Avoidance.

Persistent Drive for Autonomy.

Same three letters. But listen to where each description puts your attention.

One can leave an adult watching for the thing a child is refusing. The other makes me ask where the child has room to make a decision, get what they need and feel some ownership of what happens next.

That is why I prefer Persistent Drive for Autonomy. As an educator and a neurodivergent person, I want language that helps me understand the person in front of me and do something useful with that understanding.

“What changes when we start with autonomy?”

Take the student who has forgotten their music. They need a copy. We can make that a public conversation about responsibility, or they can quietly get one from the master binder and join the rehearsal.

The second option still involves responsibility. The student notices the problem and uses a resource to solve it. The adults prepared the environment and taught them how it works.

Nobody needed a small ceremonial lecture first.

When I start with autonomy, I look for those opportunities. Where can the student make a decision? What can they reach without asking? Which parts of the situation have become a negotiation that does not need to exist?

In math, it might be spare worksheets in a known place. In another class, it might be written instructions the student can check again. They can keep moving because the support is available.

“But what if they want to do it and still can’t?”

That is part of why the word avoidance can leave people with the wrong picture. It is easy to hear it as a deliberate decision to dodge an unpleasant task.

The PDA Society’s explanation of demand avoidance includes difficulty with things a person wants to do, as well as internal expectations and changes in what they can manage. A child can want to join the ensemble and still struggle when getting started feels loaded with pressure.

Adding “But you said you wanted this” is unlikely to supply the missing support.

I would rather look at the entry point. Can they see the plan? Choose a workable starting part? Get help privately? Take an agreed break and return? We can work with the student and family to find which of those things actually makes a difference.

“Is this just nicer wording?”

It would be, if we changed the heading and carried on doing exactly the same thing.

A beautifully printed autonomy poster does not make a charger accessible. It does not teach a routine, change the sound in the room or give a student a usable way to ask for help.

The words matter to me because they change the questions I want educators to ask. Then the classroom has to answer those questions with something the student can use.

Autonomy can include choosing help. It can include an adult sitting alongside someone, modelling the first step or offering two clear options. Supporting a student to support themselves takes adult work, especially at the beginning.

“Does everyone prefer this name?”

People have different relationships with these terms. Some find the original wording useful for explaining how disabling their experiences can be. Others prefer demand-avoidant language or simply PDA. I can explain my preference without taking somebody else’s words away from them.

The Autism Education Trust’s terminology guide, co-produced with autistic young people, includes discussion of Persistent Drive for Autonomy and discomfort with the original terminology. That is a useful place to hear perspectives beyond mine.

You will still see Pathological Demand Avoidance in research titles and linked resources. I keep those titles intact so you can find and read the original work. The discussion about how PDA is understood and assessed continues in the research.

For my teaching, Persistent Drive for Autonomy points me towards the work I can do: reduce unnecessary pressure, make support reachable and give the learner room to act.

I want the conversation to begin with “What could make this manageable for you?”
