The Voice That Doesn’t Stop

“Why am I still replaying that conversation?” If your brain thinks it’s getting royalties, pull up a chair. Let’s talk about getting stuck and finding some room.

By Sarah Batchelor · Draft created · Sources checked 25 November 2026

The conversation ended hours ago. Your brain has apparently scheduled six follow-up meetings, none of which you agreed to attend.

That description will be familiar to some readers. Other people think mainly in images, sensations or different combinations. There is no standard-issue neurodivergent internal monologue.

What interests me here is what happens when an experience keeps demanding attention long after the adults consider it finished.

“We’ve already talked about this” may not finish the conversation

Imagine a student who keeps returning to an incident at lunch. The adult has explained the rule. The student still doesn’t know whether they are in trouble tomorrow, whether the other person understood them or what will happen next.

Repeating the rule may answer a question nobody is asking.

A more useful response might clarify the missing information: “You’re not in trouble. We’ll speak privately after lunch tomorrow. You can write down what you want me to understand.” That gives the student something concrete to refer to.

We can be clear about what is known and what still needs sorting out. “Don’t worry about it” is considerably less helpful when nobody has explained what “it” now means.

Give the thought somewhere to go

A student might find it easier to leave a written question in an agreed place than interrupt a lesson or explain it aloud. A short note, a drawing or a discreet signal can provide a route to help.

The arrangement needs to be real. Who checks it? When can the student expect a response? A suggestion box nobody opens is a stationery-based disappointment.

For another student, an accessible plan may answer the question without another conversation. In rehearsal, a visible order of pieces lets them check what is coming. A marked change on their part is more useful than remembering a correction delivered while twenty other things were happening.

These are possibilities to discuss, not a requirement to put every feeling on paper. The point is to make communication easier, including when speaking is difficult.

Reflection and getting stuck are different

Thinking about a mistake can help us solve a problem. Thinking “Why am I always like this?” for the rest of the evening may leave us with much less to work with.

Watkins’ review of repetitive thought explains that repetition can be constructive or unconstructive, depending on its form and context. Enjoying the same idea or returning to a favourite topic is not automatically a problem.

Nor can we decide what someone is thinking by how quietly they sit. A student who looks settled may still have a very busy afternoon happening internally.

Where music might fit

A familiar musical activity may offer something else to attend to. Could a student choose a short instrumental break, a creative task or quiet listening without first explaining the whole situation?

Research comparing sad and happy music found differences in mind-wandering and associated brain activity. It did not identify a playlist that fixes rumination. Ask what the experience does for this person.

A song might help them feel understood. It might also keep an upsetting memory close. Genre alone cannot make that decision for us.

Leave less material for the shame committee

Specific feedback gives a student information: “You came in two beats early.” “You always do this” gives them a much larger statement to carry home.

After a misunderstanding, a clear explanation and a chance to repair can matter. An immediate public debrief may create more to replay later.

If repetitive thoughts are affecting daily life, sleep or safety, involve the appropriate qualified support team. In the classroom, we can keep working on clear communication, accessible help and fewer reasons for a student to wonder whether one difficult moment has changed how we see them.

Sources and evidence limits

  • Taruffi et al. (2017). Effects of Sad and Happy Music on Mind-Wandering and the Default Mode Network. Scientific Reports, 7, 14396. Laboratory experiments
