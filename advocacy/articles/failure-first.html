Learning Without Shame: Why Failure First Matters

“What if ‘try again’ feels like one demand too many?” Let’s explore PDA, pressure and autonomy and how music can offer space to get things wrong, find support and try again.

By Sarah Batchelor · Draft created · Sources checked 25 November 2026

A wrong note is information.
It is not a character reference.

Mistakes happen. In music, they happen out loud.

We don’t usually ask students to perform their math homework for an audience. In music, we learn in front of other people. The missed entrance and the beautifully played phrase are both there for everyone to hear. That can feel pretty exposed.

If we want students to take musical risks, we have to make it safe to get things wrong. And we have to teach them what to do next. “Be resilient” is not an instruction manual.

Music gives us a particularly visible place to teach that response. We hear what happened, try an adjustment and hear the difference. We can practise the process together, over and over.

That gives us something to build on in academic classes when teachers are willing and able to provide the support there, too. For me, supporting a student with PDA means creating opportunities for autonomy and reducing the pressure they may already be battling internally. Being able to quietly get what they need, make a choice or pause can mean less of an internal struggle before they tackle the situation in front of them.

Kids aren’t born knowing how to handle mistakes, feedback or success. Those are skills we need to teach, right alongside the notes.

“You’re wearing a red shirt.”

This is how I introduce the idea. I choose a student I know is comfortable being singled out for a lighthearted example.

“You’re wearing a red shirt. Does that mean you have a good sense of fashion? A bad sense of fashion? Neither. It tells us you’re wearing a red shirt.”

Facts don’t always need a good-or-bad label. Sometimes they can just be facts.

“So when I say, ‘That should be F-sharp,’ am I saying you’re a terrible musician? No. I’m saying it should be F-sharp. Fix it. We have things to play.”

The correction is about the note. It is not a review of your entire personality.

“As your director, it is literally my job to correct you until you can correct yourself. So yes, I’m going to point out mistakes. A lot. I’m going to make mistakes, too, and we’re going to acknowledge those as well. We can’t improve something if we won’t acknowledge what happened.”

That student’s comfort matters. If the attention would be unwelcome, use yourself as the example. An introduction to removing judgement should not become an uncomfortable spotlight.

Why this matters for students with PDA

For a student with a PDA profile, “try again” can feel like another demand. Add an audience, an immediate expectation and an adult waiting for a response, and we may be adding pressure when we think we’re helping.

That’s why I want us to think about how feedback lands. Could it be private? Would a cue help? Does the student need a smaller step, a choice or a pause?

A correction should give them something they can use. It shouldn’t require them to prove they’re fine before support becomes available.

These ideas can help many learners. Useful feedback, less shame and room to ask for help are good things to build into a classroom. But that doesn’t mean everyone needs the same support. A student’s particular sensory or communication needs still matter.

We practise what happens next

Failure First means mistakes are normal and expected. We acknowledge them, work out what they tell us and practise responding.

“I missed the entrance” gives us something to investigate. “I’m terrible at music” gives us a whole other problem.

Maybe we mark the rest. Maybe we rehearse a smaller section. Maybe today we pause and come back. The point is to teach a response the student can actually use.

Success needs a response, too. We can notice what helped and enjoy the progress without making one good attempt a promise that every future attempt will be perfect.

And the adults need to model this. “I gave that cue too late. Let me change it.” We can hardly ask students to acknowledge mistakes while pretending we never make any.

Supporting students to support themselves

A student forgets their music. They quietly grab the master binder, find their part and carry on with the day.

They’ve noticed a problem and found a way to deal with it. The support is almost invisible. No public explanation. No need to ask permission. No lecture about remembering their music next time.

They know they forgot it. That information is not missing.

For me, supporting a student with PDA means helping them support themselves through the situation. Often, that starts with us changing the environment so they can access what they need.

The master binder gives the student a way to act. They can solve the problem without first having to announce it to the room. That is something we can build on in other classes.

What could this look like in math? If a student forgets a worksheet or textbook, is there a known place to grab an extra copy without asking? If their laptop battery dies, is there a shared charger they can use?

And does that charger come with a “Next time, remember your own”?

Because the charger is useful. The commentary is considerably less so.

A student may already be embarrassed about forgetting something. A reminder in front of the class can turn a small, solvable problem into an opportunity for shame. Now they’re dealing with that as well as the missing worksheet.

A known place for spare materials and a shared charger, available to everyone, can make this ordinary classroom business. Students can get what they need and get back to learning without having to explain why they deserve the help.

That is how I would connect the experience across settings: a familiar way to notice a problem, access a resource and continue. The student gets more opportunities to support themselves because the adults have made that possible.

If it works in band and falls apart in math, look at what is different. Can they access the resource as easily? Do they have to ask? Does using it draw attention? Is a correction or a lecture attached?

Sometimes the most useful thing we can teach is “There’s a way to sort this out.” Then we need to make sure there actually is.

Where the research fits

Classroom error-climate research, error management training and self-compassion experiments offer related evidence about responding to mistakes and learning from them. They inform my approach; they don’t independently validate Failure First.

Research on broad transfer from music training also gives us reason to be careful about promises. Here, I’m proposing that we deliberately teach and practise a response across settings. Music alone doesn’t guarantee that transfer.

A conversation worth having

For parents and educators, a useful starting question is: “What happens when this child gets something wrong?”

Bring what you each know. Ask what makes feedback easier to use and which supports could carry across settings. Agree on something practical to try.

The F-sharp still matters. So does the child playing it.
