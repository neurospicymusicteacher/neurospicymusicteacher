Maslow Before Bloom

“Lovely lesson plan. Has everyone eaten?” Let’s talk about the human needs hiding behind the learning objectives.

By Sarah Batchelor · 25 November 2026

The learning objective is beautifully written on the board. The student is hungry, the chair is uncomfortable and the percussion section has just discovered how loudly a cymbal can be played.

We may need to attend to more than the objective.

“Maslow before Bloom” is shorthand I use to remember the human being doing the learning. It should prompt a useful adjustment, rather than become another poster everyone walks past.

What can the student access without a performance?

Imagine a student who needs water, a quieter spot or a short break. Can they use an agreed routine, or do they need to explain their discomfort in front of the room?

Could hearing protection be in a known place? Could a visible plan tell them when the loud section is coming? Is there a clear way to get help if the usual arrangement isn’t enough?

These are details a student and school team can work out together. They can turn “I need something” into an action the student can take, with adult support when needed.

For a student with a PDA profile, reducing the number of requests and negotiations may itself be worth exploring. Having access to a support is different from knowing that one exists somewhere, behind an adult’s permission.

Support does not require a perfectly settled child

A student can be unsettled and still want to compose something ambitious. They can need movement and still have musical ideas worth developing. Needing support does not automatically mean needing easier work.

Nor should students have to demonstrate perfect calm before they are offered the activity that helps them participate. That is a fairly impressive circular arrangement, but not a particularly useful one.

We can keep the learning goal and change how the student reaches it. A different seat, a smaller group or a private recording might help with one task. Another task might require different instruction or a different time.

Music is not automatically the calm room

Music can offer comfort and connection. It can also be loud, unpredictable and socially demanding. A room full of instruments does not come with a universal regulation guarantee.

A predictable cue before a loud entrance gives a learner time to prepare. Access to hearing protection lets them act on that information. A quieter participation option may keep the musical learning reachable.

The important bit is asking whether the arrangement works for the student. Our favourite calming song may not be theirs. Quiet is allowed to win.

About that famous hierarchy

Maslow’s original 1943 paper describes relative satisfaction of needs and exceptions to a fixed order. Bloom’s taxonomy concerns educational objectives. Combining the names does not create a tested biological sequence for learning.

I am using the phrase as an educational reminder. Students do not have to achieve a perfectly completed set of human needs before they can learn something interesting.

Bring one concrete question to the meeting

“What could they access more easily without having to ask every time?” may get us further than “How do we motivate them?”

A family might explain that transitions are exhausting. A teacher might identify an earlier arrival option or a clearer setup routine. Together, they can choose something feasible, agree on who will make it happen and ask the student whether it helps.

A pyramid cannot do that part for us.
