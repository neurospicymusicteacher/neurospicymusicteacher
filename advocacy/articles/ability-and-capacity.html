When Ability Isn’t Capacity

“But they did it yesterday.” Great. Today has entered the chat. Let’s unpack why ability and capacity aren’t the same thing.

By Sarah Batchelor · 25 November 2026

“But they did it yesterday.”

Excellent. We have information about yesterday.

Now we need to find out what is happening today.

I use ability to mean a learned skill, and capacity to mean the resources someone has available to use it in this situation. We have to ask and observe. There is no helpful battery icon floating above the child’s head.

Count the steps hiding inside the task

“Get ready for band” might mean finding music, assembling an instrument, changing rooms, coping with noise, remembering instructions and working out where everyone has started.

The student may be able to play the passage. Getting to the point where they can play it is a separate collection of tasks.

Imagine music and equipment laid out in predictable places, a visible rehearsal plan and the starting bar on the board. Now compare arriving to a room where every piece of information requires a question.

The musical expectation might be the same. The amount the student has to manage before reaching it is not.

Give them something they can use today

A student could have an agreed way to choose a shorter part, listen briefly before joining or start with a familiar pattern. A discreet signal might let them request adult help or a break.

These options need to be familiar enough to use without a lengthy conversation at the hardest moment. A student should not need to give a persuasive presentation on their current capacity before an agreed support becomes available.

The options are also not a ladder. Listening is not the bottom rung from which someone earns the right to play. A student may move between activities or need a different kind of support altogether.

Keep the learning goal clear

If the goal is rhythmic understanding, tapping a pattern or arranging beats digitally might show it. If the goal is instrumental technique, a digital arrangement does not demonstrate that same skill. We might instead adjust the setting, time or amount being assessed.

That distinction matters. Flexibility should give the learner a usable route into the learning, with teaching along the way.

For a student with a PDA profile, it may also help to discuss the options before assessment day. Having a choice already agreed can reduce the amount of negotiating required when the pressure is higher.

Quiet completion does not tell us the whole cost

Families might report that a student manages the school day and then needs hours to recover. Educators might see a change in participation after a particular transition. Both pieces of information belong in the conversation.

Ask what made a task possible and what it cost. Then look for something the adults can adjust: instructions, noise, transitions, timing or access to materials.

We don’t need to turn every difficult afternoon into a diagnosis. We do need to take changes seriously.

A word about burnout

Raymaker and colleagues’ research with autistic adults describes accounts of exhaustion, reduced tolerance to stimuli and loss of skills in autistic burnout. It is qualitative adult research. It cannot tell us why a particular child struggled on Tuesday.

A sustained change in functioning deserves attention from the family and relevant professional team. Meanwhile, the classroom question remains useful: “What support would make this more available today?”

Yesterday’s success can tell us something is possible. Today’s circumstances help us work out what needs to be alongside it.
