The Voice That Doesn’t Stop

“Why am I still replaying that conversation?” If your brain thinks it’s getting royalties, pull up a chair. Let’s talk about getting stuck—and finding some room.

By Sarah Batchelor · Draft created · Sources checked 25 November 2026

A note about my perspective: I'm a music educator, special education specialist, neurodivergent human and parent — not a medical professional, psychologist or neuroscientist. I'm just a neurospicy person with intense hyperfocus, professional and lived experience, and access to the internet.

My articles explore research through an educational and lived-experience lens. They are intended to inform, encourage curiosity and offer practical ideas, not to provide clinical advice. I encourage you to explore the original research linked throughout.

The conversation ended hours ago. Your brain appears to have scheduled six more meetings about it.

That image may be familiar to some readers. It will not describe everyone. People think in words, images, sensations and many combinations; some do not experience a continuous inner voice. Neurodivergent people do not share one thinking style.

When I talk about an internal narrative, I mean the meanings we attach to experiences: what happened, why it happened and what we believe it says about us. That is an educational way of discussing experience, not a diagnosis.

Repetition is not automatically a problem

Rehearsing a musical phrase is repetitive. Planning a difficult conversation can be useful. Returning to a favourite idea can be pleasurable. The concern is not simply that a thought repeats; it is whether the process is distressing, inflexible or keeping someone from what they need.

Watkins' review of repetitive thought distinguishes constructive and unconstructive forms and considers the importance of context and how a person thinks about an experience. It gives us a better starting point than declaring all repeated thinking unhealthy.

For a school team, “this student keeps talking about it” should open a question. Has the problem actually been resolved? Does the student need clarification, repair, predictability or support? Repeated concern about an ongoing problem is not evidence that the student is the problem.

Inner speech is not the same as rumination

An inner conversation might help someone plan, create or make sense of a day. Rumination usually refers to repetitive thinking that circles distress without moving towards a useful resolution. Worry often concerns what might happen next. The categories overlap, but using one word for everything makes the support less precise.

This article does not establish that autism or ADHD necessarily causes a particular kind of inner dialogue. Nor can a teacher identify someone's thoughts from their facial expression. A still student might be resting, concentrating, overwhelmed or somewhere else entirely.

Listening begins with accessible ways to communicate. Speaking on demand may not be the easiest route. Writing, drawing, an agreed signal or a later conversation may offer more room.

Music can change the setting for thought

Research comparing sad and happy music found differences in mind-wandering and associated brain activity. It did not show that one kind of music is a treatment, or that mind-wandering is necessarily harmful.

My practical interpretation is to ask how a musical activity affects the person using it. Does it help them feel understood? Does it intensify an upsetting loop? Does playing offer a focus that listening does not? A genre label cannot answer those questions for them.

What adults can change

Use specific feedback: “The entrance needs a clearer cue” gives a learner something to work with. “You always do this” attaches the event to an identity. Avoid turning a student's difficult moment into a public account of their character.

Offer a short check-in after a misunderstanding. Explain what will happen next. Make repair possible without requiring an immediate emotional performance or an account in front of peers.

If repetitive thoughts are significantly affecting sleep, safety or daily life, involve the appropriate qualified support team. Music can remain part of a supportive school day while those needs are addressed.

The goal is not a silent mind. It is a student who has room to think, ways to seek help and fewer school experiences that tell them they are fundamentally wrong.
