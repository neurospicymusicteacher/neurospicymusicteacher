When Ability Isn’t Capacity

“But they did it yesterday.” Great. Today has entered the chat. Let’s unpack why ability and capacity aren’t the same thing.

By Sarah Batchelor · Draft created · Sources checked 25 November 2026

“But they did it yesterday.”

Yes. Yesterday is useful information. It is not a complete assessment of what is available today.

I use ability to mean a skill someone has learned, and capacity to mean their available resources for using it in a particular situation. This is a practical educational distinction, not a diagnostic test or a measurable fuel gauge inside the brain.

A student may know the fingering and still struggle to begin in a noisy room, follow a rapid instruction or tolerate the attention of a public assessment. The task includes more than the visible musical skill.

What else is the task asking?

Getting ready for band can mean finding equipment, navigating a transition, interpreting instructions, coping with sound, coordinating movement and anticipating other people's responses. “Play these four bars” may be the final request in a long chain.

Rather than assuming that inconsistent performance proves inconsistent effort, inspect the chain. Which part is difficult today? Is the music unfamiliar? Is a transition rushed? Is the instrument uncomfortable? Has the student had a chance to eat or rest?

These are questions to explore with the learner, not explanations to impose. A teacher cannot identify someone's sensory or emotional state by looking at how compliant they appear.

Fluctuation is not the same as burnout

Raymaker and colleagues' participatory research with autistic adults described exhaustion, reduced tolerance to stimuli and loss of skills in accounts of autistic burnout. This qualitative work helps foreground lived experience. It does not establish a cause for every difficult school day, nor a timetable for recovery in children.

A student can have a hard afternoon without being in burnout. A longer pattern of exhaustion or change in functioning deserves attention from the relevant family and professional team. An educational metaphor should not replace a careful understanding of the individual.

Offer more than one way in

Build a menu of participation that does not function as a ranking. Listening, exploring a pattern independently, playing a shorter part and joining an ensemble can all be meaningful choices. A student should not have to prove readiness through one role before another is available.

Keep instruction accessible within each option. Observation can include a clear listening focus if the student wants one. Independent exploration can include a visual prompt or a recording. A shorter part can still have real musical value.

These are proposed teaching adjustments. Their usefulness should be reviewed with students rather than assumed because they sound inclusive.

Separate the learning goal from the obstacle

If the goal is rhythmic understanding, a student might demonstrate it by tapping, arranging beats digitally or recording a short example. If the goal specifically involves instrumental technique, a different medium may not demonstrate that same skill; an adjusted time, setting or amount may be more appropriate.

Flexibility works best when adults are clear about what is being assessed. Otherwise, an accommodation can accidentally remove instruction or hide the fact that a student needs a different kind of teaching.

Plan for the changing day

Agree on a discreet way to request a break or a different entry point. Decide who can help, where the student can go and how returning works. A flexible plan is easier to use when it does not require a persuasive speech in the hardest moment.

For families and educators, the shared question is “What would make this available today?” Keep yesterday's success as evidence of possibility. Use today's information to decide what support belongs alongside it.

Sources and evidence limits
