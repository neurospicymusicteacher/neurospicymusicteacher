Can You Worry and Sing at the Same Time?

“Can my brain’s worry committee take a coffee break?” Let’s explore singing, repetitive thoughts and what the research actually says.

By Sarah Batchelor · Draft created · Sources checked 25 November 2026

A note about my perspective: I'm a music educator, special education specialist, neurodivergent human and parent — not a medical professional, psychologist or neuroscientist. I'm just a neurospicy person with intense hyperfocus, professional and lived experience, and access to the internet.

My articles explore research through an educational and lived-experience lens. They are intended to inform, encourage curiosity and offer practical ideas, not to provide clinical advice. I encourage you to explore the original research linked throughout.

“You can't worry and sing at the same time” is a memorable sentence. It is also more certain than the research allows.

A more useful version is this: singing may give some people a temporary break from worry. That is worth exploring. We do not need to claim that a brain becomes incapable of distress the moment someone starts a chorus.

For a student replaying an awkward conversation or anticipating everything that could go wrong, a few minutes of chosen musical engagement might feel like a change of channel. For another student, singing in front of people might be the source of the worry. Both responses matter.

What the studies actually measure

Schladt and colleagues found lower reported worry after solo and choir singing in an adult choir sample. This was not a demonstration that rumination stops during singing, nor a study of students with PDA. Mood ratings after an activity cannot tell us everything about the thoughts occurring during it.

Schendel and Palmer studied how speaking or singing interfered with recognition memory. The work makes shared cognitive demands a plausible part of the explanation for a sense of mental reprieve. It did not test a rumination treatment. A memory task and an anxious thought are not interchangeable.

Those distinctions do not make music less interesting. They help us ask a better question: which musical experiences help this person, in this situation, and how can we tell?

Listening, singing and feeling better are different things

A stress-recovery experiment found improved mood during self-selected music listening, but no significant difference in rumination between its recovery conditions. Feeling better and thinking less repetitively are different outcomes.

We should not promise that a playlist will solve distress, or assume that active music-making is always superior to listening. Someone may want quiet. Someone may prefer an instrument. Someone may enjoy a familiar song but dislike being told to sing it as a regulation exercise.

The student's choice is part of the support. A compulsory musical reset can become one more demand to manage.

A small experiment schools can try

Offer an optional musical break: humming privately, singing with a trusted person, playing a familiar pattern, making a beat or listening quietly. Keep the volume comfortable and the setting predictable. Agree on how the student can decline or stop.

Beforehand, ask what they hope the break will offer: comfort, expression, energy or a little distance from a thought. Afterwards, an optional check-in might be “More settled, the same or more stirred up?” There is no need to demand disclosure of the thought itself.

Notice delayed effects, too. A student may enjoy an activity yet feel exhausted by the social demands around it. A support should be judged by the student's experience, not just by whether they became quieter.

Keep the bigger problem in view

Music cannot remove bullying, make an inaccessible classroom accessible by itself or replace appropriate mental health support. If the same situation keeps causing distress, change the situation as well as offering ways to cope.

My educational interpretation is that music can be one option in a student's collection of supports. It is an invitation to explore, not a promise to switch off a brain. And if the student says “That doesn't help me,” that is useful information, too.

Sources and evidence limits

  • Schladt et al. (2017). Choir versus Solo Singing: Effects on Mood, and Salivary Oxytocin and Cortisol Concentrations. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 11, 430. Adult singing study
  • Schendel & Palmer (2007). Suppression effects on musical and verbal memory. Memory & Cognition, 35, 640–650. Three laboratory experiments
  • Radstaak et al. (2014). Music and psychophysiological recovery from stress. Psychosomatic Medicine, 76, 529–537. Randomized experiment
