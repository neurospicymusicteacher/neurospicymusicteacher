Understanding PDA: A Persistent Drive for Autonomy

“What if ‘just do it’ isn’t helping?” Let’s talk demands, autonomy and what support can look like when more pressure isn’t the answer.

By Sarah Batchelor · Draft created · Sources checked 25 November 2026

“Just ask them to do it.”

Sometimes the asking is part of what makes it difficult.

That can be hard to understand when the task looks simple from the adult side. Find the sheet. Start the activity. Answer the question. How many demands can be hiding in one apparently straightforward instruction?

What I mean by PDA

PDA commonly stands for Pathological Demand Avoidance. Some people prefer Persistent Drive for Autonomy. The latter is a community reframing, not an established scientific definition.

PDA is not a standalone diagnosis in DSM-5 or ICD-11. Its boundaries and assessment remain contested. Families also use different language for their experiences. Ask what is helpful to the person you’re supporting.

A label cannot tell us exactly why a student is struggling. It can open a conversation. It should not close one.

Find the demands we can actually change

Imagine a student arriving late to a lesson. The instructions have been spoken, the worksheet is in the teacher’s cupboard and everyone else has started.

Before doing the task, they need to interrupt, explain themselves, request the sheet and ask what to do. We may be watching an unopened exercise book and calling the problem motivation.

A visible starting point, accessible spare sheets and an example to refer to could remove several of those steps. The student can begin without a public account of how they got into this situation.

That is an environmental adjustment worth exploring. It is not proof of a particular diagnosis or cause.

Autonomy needs real options

In music, could a learner choose between two workable starting tasks? Could they access their part without asking? Is there an agreed way to pause and return?

“If you don’t do this, you’ll lose something important” contains a choice in the grammatical sense. I would not describe it as an exciting expansion of autonomy.

The options need to be honest. If one task is essential, explain that plainly and explore how it can be approached. Vague hints are not automatically kinder or more accessible than a clear instruction.

And if the student needs adult help, that remains an option. Supporting autonomy does not mean expecting them to handle everything alone.

Make fewer things depend on negotiation

Consider a rehearsal where a student can see the plan, find a spare pencil, use their agreed hearing protection and locate their music without a separate request for each item.

They have more room to manage the musical task because fewer ordinary needs depend on an adult saying yes. The student and family can help identify which points of friction matter most.

We also need to check whether an adjustment helps. A menu of choices can be useful for one person and exhausting for another. Two clear options may be better than “You can do absolutely anything!” followed by sixteen questions.

Boundaries still need to make sense

Safe equipment use, manageable sound levels and other people’s access matter. Explain the boundary and offer workable choices within it. If something goes wrong, attend to safety and support repair when the student can take part.

There is little benefit in requiring a detailed explanation from someone who is already overwhelmed, then treating their difficulty explaining as a second offence.

What the evidence allows us to say

The 2021 review by Kildahl and colleagues and a 2026 review of diagnostic evidence identify substantial limitations in how PDA has been studied and assessed. They do not establish these classroom adjustments as a proven PDA treatment.

The practical suggestions here are proposals to discuss and review with an individual learner. A useful school conversation might begin: “Having to request every resource seems to add pressure. Could we try making those materials available directly and see whether it helps?”

That gives the team something concrete to work on together.
