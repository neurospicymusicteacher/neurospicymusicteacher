Why Does Your School Need Music?

“What could music make possible here?” A case for starting, expanding and opening up music programs for neurodivergent learners and everyone else.

By Sarah Batchelor · Draft created · Sources checked 25 November 2026

Music does not need to improve a math score to deserve a place in school.

A student learning to shape a phrase, build a beat or contribute to an ensemble is learning something worthwhile. We don’t need to smuggle that value in under the heading “Also useful for fractions.”

There is interesting research on other benefits. We can use it without promising that a ukulele will resolve every difficulty in the timetable.

Start with the experience the student can actually reach

Imagine a learner who loves making beats but finds a crowded, noisy ensemble difficult to enter. A small production task, accessible headphones and clear instructions could offer a way into musical learning.

Another student might want the ensemble, with an adapted part and a predictable rehearsal plan. Digital work is not the designated neurodivergent alternative. It is one possible route among others.

Can the student find their materials, adjust within agreed sound limits and get help when they need it? Does participation depend on a public audition or an explanation of every difficulty?

The setup matters. An excellent program that a child cannot access remains fairly theoretical for that child.

What can we ask the research to carry?

A 2024 meta-analysis found benefits for inhibitory control across controlled longitudinal music-training studies. That is a specific finding, not a promise about every executive function or everyday situation.

Habibi and colleagues’ longitudinal research review describes changes in musically relevant auditory skills and associated brain development. Brain differences do not automatically mean better grades.

A review of music interventions and stress found average benefits across varied settings. It does not tell us that this rehearsal, at this volume, will feel regulating to this student.

These findings can inform a conversation. They cannot replace the student’s account of what happens in the room.

Include the less convenient findings

Sala and Gobet’s meta-analysis challenges broad claims about cognitive and academic transfer when study quality is taken into account.

It belongs beside the positive findings. We are asking different questions when we examine a specific inhibition task, general attainment or a student’s experience of belonging. One impressive headline should not be made responsible for all three.

Music still has educational value if a promised improvement elsewhere fails to appear.

Music education and music therapy have different jobs

The 2022 Cochrane review found probable benefits of music therapy for overall improvement and quality of life among autistic participants, with uncertain effects on social interaction and communication. Those findings concern music therapy, not ordinary band instruction.

Clinical goals call for appropriately qualified music-therapy expertise. Musical learning calls for skilled teaching and accessible participation. A student may have reasons to use both services.

Bring a proposal the team can discuss

A family might say: “Our child enjoys music. Could we explore a small, supervised opportunity, with a clear way to access materials and step back when needed?”

The school can help identify a feasible activity, adult and time. Together, they can check equipment, cost, transport, sensory demands and the purpose of the opportunity.

For a student with a PDA profile, useful choices and fewer permission hurdles may be part of that plan. These are proposed educational adjustments, not a proven music treatment for PDA.

Then ask whether it helps. Is the student learning musically? Do they want to return? Can they get what they need? What does the experience cost them afterwards?

Quieter behaviour and better attendance may be information. They are not the entire review. The musician having the experience should get a say in what counts as useful.

Sources and evidence limits

  • Jamey et al. (2024). Does music training improve inhibition control in children? A systematic review and meta-analysis. Cognition, 252, 105913. Systematic review and meta-analysis
  • Habibi et al. (2018). Music training and child development: A review of recent findings from a longitudinal study. Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences. DOI: 10.1111/nyas.13606. Review of a longitudinal program
  • de Witte et al. (2020; first online 2019). Effects of music interventions on stress-related outcomes: A systematic review and two meta-analyses. Health Psychology Review, 14, 294–324. Systematic review and meta-analyses
  • Geretsegger et al. (2022). Music therapy for autistic people. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, CD004381. Cochrane systematic review
  • Sala & Gobet (2020). Cognitive and academic benefits of music training with children: A multilevel meta-analysis. Memory & Cognition, 48, 1429–1441. Meta-analysis
