Why Does Your School Need Music?

“What could music make possible here?” A case for starting, expanding and opening up music programs for neurodivergent learners and everyone else.

By Sarah Batchelor · 25 November 2026

“Could music help this child?”

That’s a reasonable question. But I want us to ask another one alongside it: “What is this child missing when music isn’t available to them?”

The chance to play something they love. To make a sound with other people. To be the person who knows how that part goes. To discover that the thing they made is worth hearing.

Those are reasons to offer music education. The child does not have to produce a higher math mark to justify the instrument.

And yet, when families ask for music, the conversation can become about whether their child is ready. Can they sit still? Follow every direction? Remember their equipment? Handle the noise without support?

We’ve managed to turn access to learning into a test of whether someone can already manage the learning environment. Bit of a problem there.

“Do they need to be ready, or do we need to be ready for them?”

Think about the student who arrives without their music.

There can be a public conversation about responsibility. Or there can be a master binder they quietly grab a copy from so they can get on with playing. Same missing music. Very different afternoon.

The second option gives the student something to do about the problem. They can support themselves because someone made that possible. The adult work happened beforehand: preparing the binder, showing everyone where it lives and making it available without commentary.

For a student with a PDA profile, that difference matters. Getting a needed resource without asking permission can remove a demand and an audience from an already difficult moment. It won’t resolve every barrier. It does give them somewhere to start.

I want more of that in schools. Spare worksheets. Shared chargers. Instructions you can check without announcing that you missed them. Music gives us a place to practise building those supports, and other teachers can make the same routes available in their rooms.

“But music is noisy.”

Yes. I am a band teacher. This has come to my attention.

A room full of beginners can be a lot. Loving music does not mean loving every sound, at every volume, from every direction. A student can desperately want to be in band and still find the room overwhelming.

So we look at the room. Where can they sit? Do they know when the loud section is coming? Can they take an agreed break and return without a public explanation? Could a smaller rehearsal help them learn a part before the whole ensemble joins in?

And we ask the student. Sending the child who wants to play trumpet off to make beats on a computer might solve an adult’s scheduling problem. It hasn’t necessarily answered the child’s request.

Digital music is a worthwhile musical choice. So is trumpet. Support should help students reach the music they want to learn.

“I brought research. I also brought some perspective.”

There are findings worth sharing when we advocate for school music. Habibi and colleagues describe musically relevant auditory development and associated brain changes in their longitudinal research. A 2024 meta-analysis found benefits for inhibitory control across controlled longitudinal music-training studies.

That gives us something specific to discuss. It doesn’t mean band will fix executive functioning across a child’s entire day.

Research on music interventions and stress also reports average benefits across varied settings. The student covering their ears during rehearsal still needs us to respond to the noise. A positive research finding cannot tell us how that room feels to them.

And Sala and Gobet’s meta-analysis challenges broad promises about improvements in general cognition and academic attainment. Music education deserves support even when those promises don’t hold up.

We also need to be careful with music-therapy research. Those studies involve music therapists working towards therapeutic goals. As a music educator, I’m making a case for children to have access to musical learning and the support they need to take part.

“What if our school doesn’t have a program?”

Then families are asking for something bigger than a different seat in an existing class. They are asking the school to make musical learning available.

That deserves a real conversation about teaching time, a qualified educator, instruments or technology, space and funding. A lunchtime club may offer a welcome beginning. It doesn’t automatically provide the same access as a timetabled class, especially if a student needs lunch to recover or transport means they cannot stay late.

If we start small, we can still plan for who gets included. Who can afford the instrument? Who needs one supplied? Can a beginner join without private lessons? Who will help a learner enter the room and find their way into the music?

Putting “everyone welcome” on the door is a lovely start. We need to make it true on the other side.

Music is a subject children deserve to learn. It can also be a place where they experience adults making support available, mistakes being workable and their contribution mattering.

That is worth building a program for.
