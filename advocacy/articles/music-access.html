Music Is a Support, Not a Reward

“If music helps, why does it have to be earned?” Let’s explore how to advocate for access and bring that question into a school conversation.

By Sarah Batchelor · 25 November 2026

“You can go to music when your other work is finished.”

That sentence deserves a closer look if music is one of the things helping a student access their school day.

Music might be a curricular subject, a chosen activity, part of a support plan or clinical music therapy. Those purposes differ. Getting clear about the purpose helps us decide how access should work.

Write down more than “music break”

Imagine a plan that gives a student access to a supervised music space at an agreed time. Do they know where to go? Does the adult there know the arrangement? Is there a simple way to signal that they need it?

What happens when that adult is absent or the room is being used for something else?

A support that only works when everyone remembers the unwritten arrangement is going to have some difficult Tuesdays.

A named coordinator, a clear routine and a workable backup can make access less dependent on the student negotiating successfully when things are already hard.

Remove the extra jobs attached to getting help

Could a student use the agreed signal without describing their distress in front of peers? Can they find an instrument or headphones in a known place? Are the expectations for volume, supervision and returning clear?

Those details can help someone use support before the situation becomes overwhelming. For a student with a PDA profile, they may also reduce the pressure of repeatedly seeking permission.

And if the student needs an adult to help them get there, that counts as support, too. “Support yourself” should not become a new test of independence.

Access can disappear in very ordinary ways

The club is free, but instruments cost money. Rehearsal is open to everyone, but it starts before the student’s transport arrives. The room is available, but nobody has explained how to enter it once the session has begun.

These are practical barriers a family and school can identify together. An option on a timetable is not necessarily an option the learner can use.

Ask about equipment loans, fees, timing and entry routines. A small adjustment may make an existing opportunity much more reachable. Staffing and supervision need to be workable for the school as well.

Keep musical learning inside the opportunity

An adapted part should still have musical value. A recording or visual cue should help a student access instruction. A production or backstage role should come with teaching and genuine choice.

A child who chooses a technical role now can still want to play later. We don’t need to assign a permanent musical identity based on what was manageable one afternoon.

CAST’s Universal Design for Learning Guidelines offer a framework for building options into learning. They are design guidance, not evidence that a specific music support will work for every student.

Agree on what happens when things go wrong

Safe equipment use, appropriate sound levels and supervision matter. A clear boundary gives the team something to plan around. If one activity cannot proceed safely, what other route could meet the need?

For an agreed support, losing access because unrelated work is unfinished may undo the purpose of the plan. Families can ask how that access will be protected and who will help if the arrangement breaks down.

This is an educational argument for clear, accessible support. Particular legal entitlements depend on the setting and individual circumstances.

Start with something possible

A short pilot can give the student and team a chance to explore an opportunity without pretending the first plan will be perfect.

Agree on choices, responsibilities and a review date. Include the student’s experience and any exhaustion afterwards. Change what isn’t helping.

The goal is a student who knows there is a way to get what they need, and adults who have done the practical work to make that true.
