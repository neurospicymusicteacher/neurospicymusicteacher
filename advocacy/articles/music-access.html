Music Is a Support, Not a Reward

“If music helps, why does it have to be earned?” Let’s explore how to advocate for access and bring that question into a school conversation.

By Sarah Batchelor · Draft created · Sources checked 25 November 2026

A note about my perspective: I'm a music educator, special education specialist, neurodivergent human and parent — not a medical professional, psychologist or neuroscientist. I'm just a neurospicy person with intense hyperfocus, professional and lived experience, and access to the internet.

My articles explore research through an educational and lived-experience lens. They are intended to inform, encourage curiosity and offer practical ideas, not to provide clinical advice. I encourage you to explore the original research linked throughout.

If music helps a student access the school day, what happens when the school takes it away because the day has been difficult?

That is the question behind this part of the advocacy hub. Music can be a subject, a source of belonging and, for some students, part of a support plan. Those roles deserve clear thinking before access becomes a bargaining chip.

I am making an educational and ethical argument here. This article does not establish a legal entitlement to a particular program. School obligations and individual plans depend on the relevant setting. It does argue for describing a support clearly enough that everyone knows how it is meant to work.

Put the purpose in the plan

“Music time” can mean several different things. Is the student receiving curriculum instruction, choosing a leisure activity, using an agreed regulation break or receiving clinical music therapy? The purpose affects staffing, assessment and how the opportunity should be protected.

An accommodation should not quietly become a prize. If a student needs a planned break to participate in learning, requiring completed work before the break may defeat its purpose. If music is a curricular subject, removing it to finish unrelated work also has an educational cost.

Ask the team to name that cost rather than treating music as empty time available for other priorities.

Access has practical details

A school might offer a club but charge fees the family cannot manage. An ensemble might welcome everyone but rehearse before accessible transport arrives. A room might be available but too loud, crowded or unpredictable for the student who needs it.

Ask about instruments, fees, transport, hearing protection, communication, staffing and timing. Ask whether beginners can enter later, whether auditions are necessary and whether performance is the only way to demonstrate learning.

CAST's Universal Design for Learning Guidelines offer a design framework for providing options in engagement, representation and action or expression. They are guidance for planning, not a trial demonstrating a particular music support's effectiveness.

Build choices with musical substance

Adapt parts while continuing to teach. Offer accessible notation, a recording, a smaller section or a different setting when those changes fit the goal. Provide meaningful options such as composition or production without assuming they suit every neurodivergent learner.

Technical roles should come with instruction and choice. They should not become a permanent assignment for students whom adults have decided are unsuitable performers. Access includes room to change interests and take on new challenges.

Safety is a planning question

Students and adults need safe sound levels, equipment handling and supervision. State those requirements plainly and plan for them. If a particular activity cannot proceed safely, provide an alternative route to the relevant support where possible and review what needs to change.

That differs from withdrawing music to punish unrelated behaviour. Document who is responsible, how the student requests access and what happens when the usual adult or room is unavailable.

Start small and review honestly

A six-week pilot can make a proposal concrete. Choose a workable opportunity, agree on the student's choices and set a review date. Include the student's experience and any delayed exhaustion reported at home.

If it does not help, change the plan. If it helps, discuss how to sustain access without turning every visit into a negotiation. A support should be available when it is needed, and music education should remain an opportunity to learn, create and belong.
