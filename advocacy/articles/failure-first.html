Learning Without Shame: Why Failure First Matters

“What if ‘try again’ feels like one demand too many?” Let’s explore PDA, pressure and autonomy—and how music can offer space to get things wrong, find support and try again.

By Sarah Batchelor · Draft created · Sources checked 25 November 2026

A wrong note is information.
It is not a character reference.

That distinction is the starting point for my Failure First approach. We build mistakes into learning as normal, expected and useful. We remove the moral judgement that can turn “that entrance was late” into “I am a disappointment.” Then we teach what to do next.

But this matters beyond getting the right notes. For parents and educators, the bigger question is: what is a child learning about themselves when something goes wrong?

Are they learning that a mistake can be understood and worked through? That help is available? That they still belong? Or is every correction becoming another piece of evidence for “I can’t do anything right”?

Why this matters for neurodivergent learners—and beyond

For a student who experiences demands, uncertainty or public attention as pressure, a correction can involve much more than the information an adult intended to give. “Try again” may be a small request from our side of the music stand. It may not feel small from theirs.

This is particularly relevant when supporting a student described as having a PDA profile. Another instruction, a public correction or the expectation of an immediate retry may add pressure. Start by understanding the individual rather than assuming a label tells us how they feel.

My educational response is to make feedback specific, respectful and usable. Offer privacy, a clearer cue, a smaller step or time to pause when those would help. Keep the route back into learning open.

These principles have value beyond any one profile. Removing unnecessary shame, making feedback useful and providing responsive support can improve the learning environment for many learners. That does not mean everyone needs the same thing. Some students also need particular sensory, communication or other accommodations. “Helpful for everyone” should never become “so nobody needs anything different.”

How I introduce it: “You’re wearing a red shirt.”

When I introduce this idea to students, I choose a student I know is comfortable being singled out for a lighthearted example. Then I start with something ordinary:

“You’re wearing a red shirt. Does that mean you have a good sense of fashion? A bad sense of fashion? Neither. It tells us you’re wearing a red shirt.”

That’s it. A fact does not always need a good-or-bad label attached to it. Sometimes it can just be a fact.

“So when I say, ‘That should be F-sharp,’ am I saying you’re a terrible musician? No. I’m saying it should be F-sharp. Fix it. We have things to play.”

The correction is about the note. It is not a review of your entire personality.

“As your director, it is literally my job to correct you until you can correct yourself. So yes, I’m going to point out mistakes. A lot. I’m going to make mistakes, too, and we’re going to acknowledge those as well. We can’t improve something if we won’t acknowledge what happened.”

The point is not to pretend that musical accuracy doesn’t matter. The note still needs to change. The point is to stop making every correction carry a second, unnecessary message about who you are.

“That should be F-sharp” can mean exactly that. Information. Something we can work with. Not a personal attack.

As a practical consideration for other educators, this example depends on the student feeling comfortable with the attention. Use yourself as the example if that is a better fit for the group. The point is to remove judgement, so the introduction needs to feel respectful, too.

Music gives us somewhere to practise

Music offers frequent opportunities to notice something, adjust it and try again. A missed entrance can become a chance to locate the barrier, choose a cue and discover what a different attempt sounds like.

The skill we are practising is not simply accepting correction with a smile. It is noticing a difficulty without turning it into a verdict about yourself, finding support and choosing a workable next step.

My proposed routine is: notice, understand, choose, retry and reflect. A pause can belong anywhere in that process. There is no prize for pushing through overwhelm to make the adult’s lesson plan look successful.

An adult can model the same response to their own ordinary mistake. “I gave that cue too late. Let me change it.” We are asking students to acknowledge facts; we need to be willing to do that ourselves.

Taking the practice beyond the music room

If a learner experiences a mistake at a manageable level in music, with support and a route forward, that gives us an experience we can refer back to elsewhere. The aim is to help recovery become more available across situations—not to assume it automatically will.

Imagine a student who learns that slowing a difficult rhythm down helps them find a starting point. In another lesson, an adult might ask: “Remember how a smaller section helped in music? Would breaking this into one first step help here?”

Or a student who uses a private cue to ask for help in rehearsal might explore a similar signal with another teacher. These are proposed ways of connecting the learning, not guaranteed outcomes of playing an instrument.

The adults can help by agreeing on familiar language, offering the actual support and checking whether the connection makes sense to the student. Practise in different settings at a workable time, rather than introducing a new strategy in the middle of an overwhelming moment.

What we must not do is turn success in music into another accusation: “You can do it in band, so why can’t you do it here?” Different environments place different demands on a person. The useful question is: “What helped there, and what support might make this possible here?”

What the research adds—and where my interpretation begins

Classroom error-climate research links a more positive perceived climate with more constructive patterns of responding to errors over time. It was conducted in mathematics classrooms, not as a test of my music approach.

Error management training research offers related evidence about exploration, learning from errors and applying skills to unfamiliar tasks. Self-compassion experiments also challenge the assumption that harsh self-criticism is necessary for motivation to improve.

Those connections inform Failure First; they do not independently validate this particular approach. And research challenging broad transfer from music training is a good reminder not to promise that musical participation alone will produce wider cognitive or academic gains. The proposed connection here involves explicitly supporting and practising a response across settings.

What families and educators can ask together

“What happens when this child gets something wrong?” is a useful place to begin. So are “What makes feedback easier to use?” and “Which recovery strategies could we support consistently?”

Parents can contribute what their child says and experiences outside school. Educators can contribute what they notice about tasks, instruction and the learning environment. The aim is a shared understanding and a practical support to try, rather than a script every school must follow.

The note can still need to change. A safety boundary can still matter. We can keep expectations, teach skills and support repair without turning mistakes into judgements about a child’s worth.

My hope is that the practice starts to offer a more useful internal sentence: “That was difficult. I can ask for help. I have options.” That matters in a rehearsal. It matters in a classroom. It matters in life.
