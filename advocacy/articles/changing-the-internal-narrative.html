Changing the Internal Narrative Through Music

“I got it wrong. Does that mean I am wrong?” Let’s explore how music can offer a different story about learning.

By Sarah Batchelor · Draft created · Sources checked 25 November 2026

There is a difference between “I played that incorrectly” and “I am the kind of person who gets everything wrong.”

The first describes an event. The second turns an event into an identity. My interest in Failure First is partly about that gap: can we design musical learning so students have repeated opportunities to separate the two?

This is an educational proposal. I am not claiming that a rehearsal routine rewires a student's self-concept or treats a mental health condition. I am asking what stories our classrooms make available to the people learning in them.

Another experience, not just another slogan

Telling someone to be confident is not the same as giving them an experience of learning with support. “Believe in yourself” can feel like another task when the environment keeps making participation difficult.

Imagine a learner who loses their place in a score. In this illustrative example, the teacher marks a visual cue, rehearses a smaller section and offers a private retry. The student now has evidence that a barrier can be identified and a strategy can help.

The potential story is more specific than “I am amazing.” It is “I can ask for a cue,” “I can change my approach” or “I can return after a difficult attempt.” Those are believable statements tied to something that happened.

What related research adds

Self-compassion experiments by Breines and Chen found greater motivation to improve after compassionate responses to shortcomings in several tasks. Those experiments do not establish long-term effects of music education on students' internal narratives. They do challenge the idea that improvement requires attacking yourself.

Classroom error-climate research also points towards the importance of the social setting around a mistake. My connection to music is an interpretation: frequent feedback and repeated attempts create opportunities to practise a different response to being unfinished.

Musical identity has more than one doorway

A student might connect with composing, keeping a pulse, choosing a sound, recording an ensemble or helping make a performance possible. Belonging should not depend entirely on being the most technically fluent performer.

Offer meaningful roles and genuine instruction in them. A technical role should be a choice with opportunities to grow, rather than a place to put students whom adults have decided cannot play. Keep the route back to instruments or singing open.

Ask the student what matters to them. A quiet contribution can be satisfying. A public celebration can be welcome or uncomfortable. The adult's preferred picture of success is not automatically the student's.

Thought content and lasting change are different

A study of heroic- and sad-sounding music found differences in the thoughts participants reported during listening. That is interesting evidence about immediate thought content. It does not prove lasting changes in self-belief or tell us to prescribe triumphant playlists.

The longer educational possibility rests on the experience we build around music: access, agency, useful feedback, repair and opportunities to contribute. A song cannot compensate for repeated humiliation in the room where it is taught.

Listen for a usable next step

After a difficulty, try “What did that attempt tell you?” If reflection is too demanding right then, offer a concrete observation and come back later. Do not insist that a student describe growth while they are still distressed.

My hope is that students leave with more than a corrected note. Perhaps they leave with a smaller, kinder and more useful sentence: “That was difficult. I have options.” We can create opportunities for that sentence without promising the same result for every learner.

Sources and evidence limits

  • Breines & Chen (2012). Self-compassion increases self-improvement motivation. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 38, 1133–1143. Four experiments
  • Dresel et al. (2025; first online 2024). Learning from errors in mathematics classrooms: Development over 2 years in dependence of perceived error climate. British Journal of Educational Psychology, 95, 180–196. Longitudinal observational study
  • Koelsch et al. (2019). Heroic music stimulates empowering thoughts during mind-wandering. Scientific Reports, 9, 10317. Laboratory listening study
