Can You Worry and Sing at the Same Time?

“Can my brain’s worry committee take a coffee break?” Let’s explore singing, repetitive thoughts and what the research actually says.

By Sarah Batchelor · 25 November 2026

“You can’t worry and sing at the same time.”

I like the possibility behind that sentence. I’m less enthusiastic about promising that a chorus can evict every anxious thought from a brain.

Singing may give some people a break from worry. For someone who is worried about singing, however, “Let’s all sing!” is a fairly questionable opening move.

So let’s start with the person, not the playlist.

A musical break should be available before the crisis

Imagine a student who finds a familiar keyboard pattern helpful when their thoughts are going in circles. Could there be an agreed time and supervised place to play for a few minutes? Could they use an established signal instead of explaining their entire emotional state in front of the class?

A familiar instrument, a clear access routine and an adult who knows the arrangement give the student something they can use. They don’t have to become visibly distressed enough to convince someone they need it.

Another student might prefer headphones and a chosen song. Someone else might want quiet. The student’s answer is more useful than our theory about what should calm them down.

Please don’t turn regulation into an audition

A low-pressure singing opportunity could mean humming alongside a trusted adult, joining an informal group or singing without a performance at the end. Instrumental playing, tapping a pattern and listening can be options, too.

If the student has to sing alone to demonstrate that they are participating properly, we have added a fairly substantial demand to their break.

For a student with a PDA profile, even a well-meant regulation instruction may add pressure. Agreeing on options when things are manageable can give them more say when things are difficult. They can also change their mind. Yesterday’s helpful song is not a lifetime contract.

Access still needs a workable plan for supervision, volume and equipment. A support is easier to use when everyone knows how it works.

Let the student decide whether it helped

A check-in can be small: “More settled, about the same or more stirred up?” A gesture or a later conversation may be easier than another question right now.

We don’t need the contents of every thought. We need to know whether the opportunity is useful, whether anything about it adds pressure and whether the student can stop.

If music makes the experience worse, that is information. It is not evidence that the student failed to regulate correctly.

What the research actually gives us

Schladt and colleagues found that adults in a choir sample reported less worry after singing alone and together. I’m interested in what that might offer students, while remembering who actually took part in the study.

Schendel and Palmer’s memory experiments explored how speaking and singing interact with memory. That helps me think about what occupies our attention while we make music; worry was a different question from the one those experiments tested.

And a stress-recovery experiment found improved mood during music listening without a significant difference in rumination between its recovery conditions. Feeling better and having fewer repetitive thoughts are different outcomes.

There is enough here to explore a useful option. There is not enough to prescribe a universal off switch.

Keep listening to what the worry is about

If the student is distressed because something harmful keeps happening, a musical break does not resolve that situation. The adults still need to address it. Persistent distress may also need appropriate professional support.

Music can be one resource the student learns to choose and use. The useful question is “Would this help you?” Then we need to make room for the answer.

Sources and Further Reading

  • Schladt et al. (2017). Choir versus Solo Singing: Effects on Mood, and Salivary Oxytocin and Cortisol Concentrations. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 11, 430. Adult singing study
  • Schendel & Palmer (2007). Suppression effects on musical and verbal memory. Memory & Cognition, 35, 640–650. Three laboratory experiments
  • Radstaak et al. (2014). Music and psychophysiological recovery from stress. Psychosomatic Medicine, 76, 529–537. Randomized experiment
