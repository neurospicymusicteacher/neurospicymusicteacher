Maslow Before Bloom

“Lovely lesson plan. Has everyone eaten?” Let’s talk about the human needs hiding behind the learning objectives.

By Sarah Batchelor · Draft created · Sources checked 25 November 2026

A beautiful lesson plan does not tell us whether a student is hungry, in pain, frightened or overwhelmed by the room where it will happen.

“Maslow before Bloom” is a useful educational shorthand for remembering the person inside the learning task. I use it as a prompt to consider conditions for participation. I do not use it as a rule that every need must be fully satisfied before meaningful learning can occur.

Students learn while life is complicated. They also deserve support while they learn. The phrase should help us provide that support, not become a reason to postpone access to interesting work.

Two frameworks, not a biological sequence

Maslow's theory concerns motivation and human needs. Bloom's taxonomy concerns categories of educational objectives. Putting the names together does not create a tested sequence for how a brain learns.

Maslow's original 1943 paper describes relative satisfaction and exceptions to a fixed order. The familiar idea of climbing a perfect staircase oversimplifies even that original theory. It is a historical framework, not a classroom screening tool or a current neuroscience finding.

The practical question is less grand and more useful: “What is making it hard for this student to access this learning right now?”

Support and learning can happen together

A student might learn while standing, moving, wearing hearing protection or taking a quieter role. A student might feel unsettled and still want to compose something ambitious. Needing support does not mean someone must be limited to easy work.

Conversely, willingness to participate does not tell us that the environment is comfortable. Ask what the experience costs. A student who completes everything may still need recovery time or a different way to take part.

My recommendation is to make support ordinary rather than a special reward for reaching the right emotional state. Water, breaks, clear communication and a trusted adult should not become prizes for finishing a worksheet.

What this means in a music room

Music can offer a route into connection or expression. It can also involve noise, proximity, uncertainty and public evaluation. The presence of instruments does not make a room regulating for everyone.

Tell students when a loud sound is coming. Offer a quieter place or hearing protection when appropriate. Give an understandable plan and a way to ask for a change. Build in choices about performing, recording or sharing privately when those options fit the learning goal.

These are teaching proposals, not findings from a trial of “Maslow before Bloom.” Each needs attention to the individual and to the practical realities of staffing and supervision.

A better opening question

Instead of beginning a meeting with “How do we motivate them?” try “What conditions help them participate?” The answer may include instruction, challenge, autonomy, a clearer relationship or a physical need that has been overlooked.

Avoid making calm appearance the admission ticket to learning. A child does not have to look settled to deserve interesting music, and an adult cannot reliably read well-being from stillness.

Keep the principle useful

Choose one barrier the team can change. Write down who will act and when the student can tell you whether it helped. Keep the learning goal alongside the support so the plan protects both access and educational opportunity.

For me, the value of this phrase is its reminder to look up from the objective and notice the learner. It works best when it leads to a concrete change, rather than a pyramid on a staffroom wall.
