When a Sticker Isn’t a Support

“What if another sticker isn’t the missing piece?” A sticker won’t make the room quieter. Let’s figure out what’s actually getting in the way.

By Sarah Batchelor · 25 November 2026

If the room hurts your ears, a sticker does not make it quieter.

I have nothing against stickers enjoying a modest career as stickers. I do have questions when we ask them to do the work of an accommodation, a clear instruction or an accessible environment.

Before adding an incentive, it helps to know what is actually in the way.

“Practise more” is not a complete plan

Imagine a student looking at a practice sheet that says “Work on the difficult bits.” They don’t know which bit to start with or what to do differently.

A reward for completing the sheet leaves those questions unanswered.

A marked passage, an example of one practice strategy and a manageable first attempt could provide something the learner can use. A reference recording might let them check independently. An adult can remain available if they need help.

Now the support addresses a barrier. We can review whether it actually helps rather than simply counting more unfinished sheets.

Ask what the chart is recording

A public chart can tell us who completed a task. It tells us considerably less about who understood the instruction, had the equipment or managed the sound level.

If a student repeatedly misses the same expectation, the chart may be giving us information about the plan. Colouring another square does not resolve that information.

A private conversation with the learner and family might identify a very ordinary adjustment: accessible spare supplies, a visual starting point or a predictable place to work.

For some students with PDA profiles, the expectation attached to a reward may also add pressure. That is something to explore with the individual, not a reason to assume every student experiences incentives in the same way.

The support should not be the prize

Suppose a student’s plan includes a quieter break. If they must finish the task before accessing it, what happens when they need the break to make the task possible?

The same question applies when music is an agreed support. Turning access into something to earn changes how the plan works.

A known break routine or an agreed signal can let a student act before things become overwhelming. They should still have access to adult help. Supporting themselves does not mean being left to struggle independently.

Safety and supervision need planning, of course. If one activity cannot happen safely, the team can work on another way to meet the need.

Praise can carry pressure, too

“The marked cue helped you find the entrance” offers useful information. A public celebration might be welcome, or it might make the next attempt feel considerably more loaded.

We can ask how a student likes feedback. Quietly? Later? Written down? An adult’s enthusiasm does not tell us how the attention feels to the child receiving it.

What the reward research says

Deci, Koestner and Ryan’s meta-analysis found that some expected tangible reward arrangements reduced measures of intrinsic motivation, while positive feedback could have different effects. The conditions and measures matter.

That does not mean every sticker is harmful, every reward is ineffective or every compliment is a problem. It means we should be careful about claiming that one reward system will solve every barrier.

The practical question is whether the student can get started, get what they need and use help without an extra helping of shame. A sticker can still be a small pleasure. It does not need to run the support plan.
