Changing the Internal Narrative Through Music

“I got it wrong. Does that mean I am wrong?” Let’s explore how music can offer a different story about learning.

By Sarah Batchelor · Draft created · Sources checked 25 November 2026

“I lost my place” and “I’m useless at this” are very different accounts of the same rehearsal.

One gives us a problem to solve. The other appears to have written a review of the entire person.

I’m interested in the experiences that make a more useful account believable. A motivational poster has its work cut out if everything else in the room says “You should already know how to do this.”

Give the student evidence they have options

Imagine a student who loses their place when the ensemble moves quickly between sections. They know how to play their part. Finding the right bar while listening, turning pages and following instructions is where things fall apart.

Clear bar numbers, a marked starting point or an agreed visual cue could give them a way back in. A reference recording might let them find the place independently when practising.

Now there is a specific experience to build on: “I lost my place, used the cue and rejoined.” That is considerably more useful than “I need to be more confident.”

The support should be available when needed. If the student has to publicly admit they are lost every time, using it may become another thing to avoid.

Notice the strategy, not a personality upgrade

“That mark helped you find the entrance” names something the learner can use again. It doesn’t turn one successful attempt into a promise that they will never lose their place again.

We can also ask how they want feedback delivered. A quiet observation may be welcome. An enthusiastic announcement to the entire room may be less of a gift than we imagined.

For a student with a PDA profile, I would also want to ask whether praise feels like encouragement or a new expectation. The label cannot answer that question for them. “Would you like feedback now or later?” can be a useful conversation when both options are workable.

Let progress belong to the learner

A student could choose to keep a short recording or note about a strategy that helped. They might compare two attempts privately, with support if they want it.

They don’t need to produce an inspirational before-and-after story for the adults. The evidence can be ordinary: a passage is easier to find, a rhythm makes sense, asking for a break didn’t end their place in the group.

Those experiences offer material for a different internal story. We cannot prescribe exactly what that story will become.

A musical identity needs room to grow

Meaningful options might include playing, composing, recording or learning sound technology. Each needs real instruction and room to develop.

The student who prefers recording today should still have a route into playing tomorrow. A technical role is a choice to explore, not a polite way of deciding someone is permanently unsuitable for the ensemble.

Families can help explain what their child values. The school can help make those opportunities reachable. Together, they can ask where the student gets to experience contribution, agency and learning with support.

The research behind the questions

Self-compassion experiments found that responding compassionately to shortcomings could support motivation to improve. Classroom error-climate research also links the social environment around mistakes with students’ responses to them. Neither independently tests this particular music approach.

A study comparing heroic- and sad-sounding music found differences in immediate thought content. That does not establish lasting changes in self-belief.

My educational proposal is to build repeated experiences of useful help, choice, repair and progress. Over time, a student may have more reasons to think “I can work with this.” We give that possibility somewhere to develop.

Sources and evidence limits

  • Breines & Chen (2012). Self-compassion increases self-improvement motivation. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 38, 1133–1143. Four experiments
  • Dresel et al. (2025; first online 2024). Learning from errors in mathematics classrooms: Development over 2 years in dependence of perceived error climate. British Journal of Educational Psychology, 95, 180–196. Longitudinal observational study
  • Koelsch et al. (2019). Heroic music stimulates empowering thoughts during mind-wandering. Scientific Reports, 9, 10317. Laboratory listening study
