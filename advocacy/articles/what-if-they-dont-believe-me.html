For parents and caregivers · Explaining PDA

“What If They Don’t Believe Me?”

“What if they think I’m making excuses?” A place to start explaining PDA, sharing useful research and asking for support the school team can put into practice.

By Sarah Batchelor · 25 November 2026

“What if they think I’m making excuses?”

You have explained what happens at home. You have described the panic, the shutdown or the child who wants to go to band and somehow cannot get through the door. And the reply is, “They seem fine here,” or, “Everyone has to do things they don’t want to do.”

It can leave a parent wondering whether they need a better explanation, a bigger pile of research or an entirely different nervous system before the next meeting.

Start smaller. You know something about your child that the team needs to hear. Let’s make that knowledge easier to work with.

“What is the part we disagree about?”

Are they unfamiliar with PDA? Unsure about the term? Seeing a different side of your child? Or agreeing that something is difficult, but unsure what support could look like?

Those are different conversations. A useful first question is: “Could you help me understand which part you’re unsure about?”

The answer helps you choose what to explain next. Sending seventeen papers to someone who needs one clear classroom example may give you eighteen things to be frustrated about.

“Can we start with what is happening?”

Describe a moment the team can picture.

“When they forget their worksheet, they have to ask for another copy in front of everyone. They get stuck, the lesson moves on and they arrive home upset about being in trouble again.”

Then add what your child tells you, if they want that shared. “They say they know they need the sheet, but asking feels impossible once everyone is watching.”

Ask the school what they notice, too. A child may show distress differently in different settings. Looking settled during the lesson and being exhausted afterwards are both things worth understanding.

We can put those observations together instead of choosing which adult gets to be correct.

“What can I say about PDA without a lecture?”

Try this:

“PDA is a term people use for a pattern of intense difficulty with demands. Some prefer Persistent Drive for Autonomy because it helps explain the importance of having a say. Even a wanted activity can become difficult when pressure gets attached. I’m asking us to look at the demands, the setting and what helps this child participate.”

The PDA Society’s explanation is a practical place to start reading about what demands can feel like. The article on why I prefer autonomy-focused language explains my own lens.

You can also be honest about the research. Kildahl and colleagues’ review brought together 13 studies and identified substantial questions about how PDA is defined and studied. Researchers have not settled every question about the profile.

We can discuss those questions and still investigate why this child is struggling to use a worksheet.

“Which research would actually help this conversation?”

Choose a source that fits the question in front of you.

For the PDA research itself, start with Kildahl and colleagues. It gives staff a way to read the evidence and its open questions directly.

For why autonomy matters, Deci and Ryan’s work on self-determination theory discusses autonomy, competence and relatedness as basic psychological needs. Autonomy includes feeling some ownership of an action. It can involve choosing help and working with other people.

For why stress can make using skills harder, Arnsten’s research review explains effects on working memory, attention and flexible thinking. This is useful background when an adult assumes a child can access every learned skill equally in a stressful moment.

For planning access, the CAST UDL Guidelines offer ways to think about different routes into learning. This is a teaching framework the team can use to plan, alongside the research and their knowledge of the student.

You can share one link and a sentence about why you chose it. A bibliography is somewhere to explore, not a reading assignment the team has to finish before the conversation can begin.

“Could we try the support and see what happens?”

Bring the conversation back to something the adults can provide.

“Could spare worksheets be available in a known place, with someone showing my child how to get one? Could we try that and check back together on whether it makes joining the lesson easier?”

Or: “My child wants to play in band. Could we discuss an instrument loan, a predictable entry routine and an adult who can help them get started?”

Agree on the change, who will make it available and a review date. Ask about learning, participation and what the experience costs the child afterwards. A quieter lesson is useful information; so is a child who gets home completely worn out.

“And if I’m still being dismissed?”

Ask who else could help work through the concern. That might be the school support coordinator, music educator or a school leader, depending on the request.

A short follow-up can keep the next step clear:

“We discussed [barrier] and [possible support]. Could you confirm what we can try, who will help and when we can review it? If that support is not workable, what other way could we make participation easier?”

You do not need to win a debate about every part of PDA to begin a practical conversation about what your child needs. Keep bringing the team back to the learner, the barrier and a usable way forward.
