“Can the Worry Committee Take a Break?”

Explore whether music gives your child some breathing room. Let them help decide.

Download the one-page handout

1. Ask what already helps.

A familiar song? A keyboard pattern? Headphones? Quiet? Singing helped reduce reported worry in one adult study, but your child gets a say in what feels useful to them.

2. Make the option easy to use.

Could there be an agreed place, time and signal for a short musical break? Sort out supervision and volume beforehand. A student should not have to explain every thought to earn a few minutes of space.

3. Check in without turning it into homework.

“More settled, the same or more stirred up?” A gesture or a later chat might be enough. They can change their mind. Yesterday’s helpful song is not a lifetime contract.

And listen to what the worry is about.

If bullying, noise or another problem keeps causing distress, adults still need to address it. A song does not fix a harmful situation. Keep other needed support available.

Words you can borrow

Could we offer [chosen option] at [time/place], with a simple way to start and stop?
